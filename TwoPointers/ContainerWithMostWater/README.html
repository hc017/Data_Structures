Container With Most Water

Day 3 · Topic: Two Pointers · Daily DSA Challenge

Difficulty: Medium Pattern: Two Pointers Time: O(n) Space: O(1)

🧩 The Problem

You're given an array height where height[i] is the height of a vertical line at position i. Pick two lines that, together with the x-axis, form a container holding the most water. 

Input:  height = [1, 8, 6, 2, 5, 4, 8, 3, 7]
Output: 49   // lines at index 1 (height 8) and index 8 (height 7): min(8,7) * (8-1) = 49

📖 The Story

Picture a row of buckets of different heights lined up on a table, and you're holding a giant sheet of plastic. You stretch the sheet between any two buckets to make a pool of water between them. The pool's water level can only ever reach the height of the shorter bucket — any higher and it just spills over that side.

The slow way: try every possible pair of buckets, measure the pool each time, and remember the biggest one. With a lot of buckets, that's a huge number of pairs to check.

The smart way: start with the two buckets at the very ends of the table — that gives you the widest possible pool to begin with. Now look at which of the two end buckets is shorter. That short one is the reason your pool is limited, so step it inward one bucket at a time, hoping to find a taller one. The taller bucket stays put, because moving it inward can only shrink your width without raising the water level (still capped by the short side). Keep stepping the shorter side inward, checking the pool size each time, until both hands meet in the middle.

That's exactly the two-pointer trick — one pointer at each end, always sliding the pointer at the shorter wall, because that's the only move that could possibly improve things.

🌍 Real-Life Example

Think of setting up a volleyball net between two poles planted at different spots along a beach, where each pole has a different height. The net can only be pulled as high as the shorter pole allows, and the area it covers depends on both the height and the distance between the poles. If you want to maximize the shaded area under the net, you'd naturally start with the two poles furthest apart, then only bother moving the shorter one closer, since the taller pole was never your limiting factor in the first place.

🔀 Logic Flow Chart

The pointers march toward each other, always shrinking the shorter wall's side, until they meet.

⚙️ Approaches Compared

ApproachTimeSpaceIdea
Brute ForceO(n²)O(1)Check every pair (i, j) and track the best area
Two Pointers (Optimized)O(n)O(1)Start at both ends, always move the shorter wall inward

Why Two Pointers wins

Single pass across the array — each pointer moves at most n times total, no nested loop needed.

Trade-off

Relies on the greedy insight that moving the taller wall can never help — worth proving to yourself once.
