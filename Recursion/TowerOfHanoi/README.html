Tower of Hanoi

Day 11 · Topic: Recursion · Daily DSA Challenge

Difficulty: Medium Pattern: Recursion / Divide & Conquer Time: O(2^n) Space: O(n)

🧩 The Problem

You have three rods — source, auxiliary, and destination — and n disks of different sizes stacked on the source rod in decreasing size order (largest at the bottom). Move the entire stack to the destination rod, obeying three rules: move only one disk at a time, only ever move the topmost disk of a stack, and never place a larger disk on top of a smaller one. 

Input:  n = 3, source = 'A', auxiliary = 'B', destination = 'C'
Output:
Move disk 1 from A to C
Move disk 2 from A to B
Move disk 1 from C to B
Move disk 3 from A to C
Move disk 1 from B to A
Move disk 2 from B to C
Move disk 1 from A to C
Total moves: 7   // formula: 2^n - 1

📖 The Story

Picture three tall pegs on a table and a stack of rings on the first peg, biggest ring at the bottom, smallest on top — like a stack of dinner plates you're not allowed to mess up. You're asked to move the whole stack to the third peg, using the middle peg as a temporary parking spot, without ever setting a bigger plate on a smaller one.

Here's the trick that makes it easy to think about: don't worry about all n plates at once. Just ask yourself — "if I already knew how to move (n−1) plates anywhere I wanted, could I solve this?" Yes! First move the top (n−1) plates out of the way onto the spare peg (pretend that's already solved), then move the one big bottom plate straight to the destination, then move those same (n−1) plates from the spare peg onto the destination, on top of the big one. Each of those "move (n−1) plates" jobs is just a smaller version of the exact same problem — so you solve it by trusting yourself to solve the smaller version, again and again, until you're down to moving just 1 plate, which is trivial.

That "trust the smaller version of the same problem" habit is exactly what recursion is. The function calls itself twice with n−1, does one simple action (move the big disk) in between, and the base case (n == 0) is what stops it from calling itself forever.

🌍 Real-Life Example

Think of packing and unpacking a set of nested moving boxes that only fit inside each other in size order — to reach the smallest box buried at the bottom of a big box on one side of the room, you first move every smaller box on top of it somewhere else, deal with the big box, then restack those smaller boxes back on top in their new spot. You never think about "all the boxes" at once — you only ever think about "the box on top" and "everything above it," which is the same recursive shrinking that solves Tower of Hanoi. This same divide-and-shrink idea also powers real backup/restore tooling, where a large archive is unpacked layer by layer instead of all at once.

🔀 Logic Flow Chart

Steps 1 and 3 are recursive calls on a smaller sub-problem (n−1 disks); step 2 is the only actual disk move performed at this level, sandwiched in between.

⚙️ Approaches Compared

ApproachTimeSpaceIdea
RecursiveO(2^n)O(n) call stackMove (n-1) disks to auxiliary, move disk n, move (n-1) disks from auxiliary to destination — trust the smaller sub-problem
Iterative (explicit stack)O(2^n)O(n) explicit stackManually simulate the recursion tree with a stack of frames & a stage counter, avoiding language call-stack limits

Why it's O(2^n)

Every disk added doubles the number of moves needed — the recursion tree has exactly 2^n - 1 "move disk" leaves.

Trade-off

The iterative version trades the elegance of recursion for a bounded, controllable amount of heap-allocated stack space.
